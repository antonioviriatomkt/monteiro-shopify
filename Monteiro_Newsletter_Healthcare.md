## This month: specifying fabric for healthcare environments

Choosing upholstery for clinical settings isn't about looks — it's about a surface that survives repeated disinfection, resists cross-contamination, and lasts through years of high-turnover use.

Get five things right and a **coated performance fabric** will almost always be the correct answer over a woven textile. Here's the short version of what to check.

### 1. Hygiene & infection control

The upholstery surface can be a reservoir for pathogens, so the single most important property is a **non-absorbent, fluid-proof surface**. Woven fabrics wick fluids deep into the fibres; a PU- or PVC-coated fabric keeps liquids on top, so they can be wiped away before they penetrate. Look for an **antimicrobial finish tested to ISO 22196 or ISO 20743** — a supplement to good cleaning, never a replacement.

### 2. Cleanability & disinfection

This is the make-or-break criterion. The question isn't "can it be cleaned?" but **"is it validated against my disinfection protocol?"** Most regimes use 70% isopropyl alcohol for routine cleaning and diluted bleach for terminal cleaning and outbreaks. Bleach is the harshest agent a coating faces — specify only fabrics that state an explicit **bleach dilution, contact time and rinse procedure**, and ask for a written cleaning instruction sheet. If a supplier can't provide one, treat it as a red flag.

### 3. Fire safety

Contract healthcare seating faces stricter rules than domestic furniture — in the UK, **BS 5852**, commonly needing a severe-hazard **Crib 5** rating. The point specifiers miss most: **fire performance is a property of the composite, not the fabric alone.** A cover that passes over one foam may fail over another — so confirm the required ignition source and get test evidence for the fabric over the intended filling.

### 4. Durability

Healthcare furniture endures heavy use and aggressive cleaning. The standard measure is the **Martindale test (EN ISO 12947)** — specify the highest abrasion class for high-turnover areas like waiting rooms and A&E. For coated fabrics, **coating adhesion and resistance to cracking and hydrolysis** often determine real-world lifespan more than the raw rub count.

### 5. Compliance & chemical safety

Vulnerable patients are in prolonged skin contact with upholstery, so the chemistry matters. Insist on **REACH conformity** and **phthalate-free construction** — and request the declarations of conformity as documentation, not just a marketing claim.

### And comfort, too

A wipe-clean surface no longer has to feel clinical. Modern coated fabrics offer a **softer, warmer hand** and a **broad colour palette** — helping create calming interiors and supporting wayfinding and dementia-friendly design.

## Your reusable specification checklist

1. Non-absorbent, fluid-proof coated surface
2. Antimicrobial finish (ISO 22196 or ISO 20743)
3. Written cleaning & disinfection instructions, incl. max bleach dilution and contact time
4. Validated compatibility with your alcohol and bleach protocols
5. Fire certification to the required BS 5852 source (commonly Crib 5) over the intended filling
6. Martindale abrasion class appropriate to traffic (EN ISO 12947)
7. REACH conformity and phthalate-free construction, with declarations supplied
8. Colour and texture options suited to the interior scheme
9. Warranty terms confirmed in writing

## Quick answers

**Can healthcare upholstery be cleaned with bleach?** Yes — if the fabric is formulated for it and you use the correct dilution and contact time, then rinse as directed.

**What Martindale rating do I need?** It depends on traffic: general contract-grade for patient rooms; the highest class available for high-turnover areas. For coated fabrics, coating durability and hydrolysis resistance matter as much as the rub count.

**Coated or woven for clinical use?** Coated, for most clinical applications — a closed, fluid-proof surface you can wipe and disinfect. Wovens still suit lower-risk, non-clinical areas.
